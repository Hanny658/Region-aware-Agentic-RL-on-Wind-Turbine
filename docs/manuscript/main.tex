\begin{abstract}
Advanced pitch controllers are normally assessed against the baseline a simulation toolbox ships with, over a few mean wind speeds, as one number per metric. Each of those steps is a choice, and this paper prices the choices. On collective pitch of the NREL~5\,MW turbine in OpenFAST we build a ladder of controllers, from the shipped gain-scheduled PI through a re-tuned one, a linear-parameter-varying MPC and a learned residual layer, evaluate every rung on the same 28 held-out 600\,s episodes at seven mean wind speeds, and then vary only the reporting convention. The aggregations are defined explicitly, and the site-weighted one is conditional on the evaluated above-rated bins rather than an annual expectation. Of the 32.3 points of improvement the full stack reaches over the shipped controller, a minimal two-parameter re-tuning of the baseline already accounts for 15.9, the model-based controller for a further 14.5, and learning for 1.9 (mean over three training seeds, spread 0.3). Holding the controllers fixed and changing only how the episodes are aggregated moves the model-based controller's advantage over the tuned baseline in power regulation from $+24.6$ points (95\,\% interval $[+23.4,+25.7]$) to $-0.3$ $[-1.7,+1.6]$, and simultaneously moves its tower-fatigue advantage from $+10.3$ $[+8.7,+12.1]$ to $+29.0$ $[+26.3,+32.2]$; both conventions are in current use. The W\"ohler exponent, by contrast, changes the magnitudes but not the ranking, which is identical at tower $m=3,4,5$ and blade $m=8,10,12$. Two design choices inside the learned layer are priced the same way: where the layer is allowed to act, and who proposes its reward. The second is a secondary finding and is reported as such, since a random search over a vocabulary distilled from the language model's own proposals matches it on performance ($+4.01$ against $+4.02$ points) and differs only on constraint satisfaction, in 4 of 5 against 1 of 8 runs. We close with the reporting practice these measurements imply, and note that no convention examined here prices the pitch activity that buys the improvement.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Above rated wind speed, collective pitch regulates rotor speed and electrical power while the tower and the blades accumulate fatigue. The classical solution is a gain-scheduled PI (GSPI) on generator speed with peak shaving~\citep{Bossanyi2000,Abbas2022rosco}; model predictive control (MPC) states the trade-off as a constrained optimisation~\citep{Soliman2011mpc,Koerber2013mpc,Schlipf2013nmpc,Evans2015rmpc,Mulders2020qlpv}; and reinforcement learning (RL) has been proposed as a model-free alternative that learns it from simulation~\citep{SierraGarcia2020,Xie2023rlpitch,Coquelet2022ipc,Baseline2026}.

A comparison between any two of these requires choices that are rarely reported as choices. Which configuration of the baseline is used, the shipped one or a tuned one. How the episodes are aggregated into a number, as a mean of per-episode percentages or as a ratio of aggregated loads. Whether the mean wind speeds are weighted equally or by a site distribution. Which W\"ohler exponent the fatigue is counted at. And, inside a learned controller, where the learning is allowed to act and who designs its reward. Each of these is defensible in more than one way, each is made in published work, and none is usually varied within a single study.

This paper prices them. We build a ladder of controllers on one plant, evaluate every rung on the same held-out simulations, and then hold the controllers fixed and vary the convention. The controllers are instruments rather than claims: each rung has to be built competently for the measurement to mean anything, but what we report is how much of the apparent benefit each choice decides.

\paragraph{Findings.} (i)~Of the 32.3 points the full stack reaches over the shipped controller on winds nothing in the pipeline has seen, 15.9 are a two-parameter re-tuning of the baseline, 14.5 are the model-based controller and 1.9 are learning, the last a mean over three training seeds whose spread is 0.3. (ii)~Changing only the aggregation and the wind weighting moves the model-based controller's power-regulation advantage over the tuned baseline from $+24.6$ points to $-0.3$, an interval that contains zero, while its tower-fatigue advantage triples from $+10.3$ to $+29.0$. (iii)~The W\"ohler exponent changes the magnitudes but not the ranking, which shows the sensitivity is specific rather than universal. (iv)~Restricting where the learned layer may act matters more than tuning the layer itself: a gate two metres per second lower costs 0.4--0.6 points on both bases and on both wind sets, and puts its worst per-wind shortfall near rated in 4 of 4 trained policies against 2 of 8. (v)~The proposer of the learned layer's reward is worth nothing in performance and, on the one base where the two can be separated, something in constraint satisfaction. (vi)~No convention here prices pitch activity, which rises from 1.14 to 2.90 times the shipped controller's travel along the ladder.

\paragraph{Scope.} One onshore turbine, collective pitch, above rated, one turbulence class. The numbers do not transfer; the accounting does.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{MPC for wind-turbine pitch.} Linear-parameter-varying and quasi-LPV formulations of the above-rated pitch problem are established~\citep{Soliman2011mpc,Koerber2013mpc,Mulders2020qlpv}, including preview~\citep{Schlipf2013nmpc} and robust variants~\citep{Evans2015rmpc,Klein2025robustmpc}, and the practical obstacles to running them on a machine are documented~\citep{Wintermeyer2023challenges}. Two elements of the controller we use exist in that literature and we claim neither. Scheduling the cost \emph{weights} on an operating variable is due to \citet{Wintermeyer2021weightsched}, who schedule $Q$ and $R$ on plant outputs varying with wind and rotor speed to smooth the partial-to-full-load transition; ours schedules a tower-fatigue weight within the full-load region. Offset-free MPC for wind energy is likewise established: \citet{Liu2024offsetfree} estimate the mismatch left by linearisation with a Luenberger observer inside a tube-based stochastic MPC, validated in FAST, comparing three MPC variants over 180\,s at three wind speeds on rated-power tracking, with no fatigue metric and no classical baseline. Closest to our model-error result is \citet{Anand2026adaptive}, who repair the model of an economic MPC for the same turbine in the same simulator with an offline-trained network and report $+9$\,\% profit.

\paragraph{Learned pitch control, and what it is compared against.} Learned collective and individual pitch controllers are reported against a PI or GSPI baseline on a small set of wind speeds~\citep{SierraGarcia2020,Xie2023rlpitch,Coquelet2022ipc,Baseline2026}; simultaneous tuning of collective and individual pitch is the classical counterpart~\citep{Lara2026simultaneous}. The closest published comparison to ours is \citet{Espinoza2026ddqn}, who train a double-deep-Q-network pitch controller in OpenFAST against the shipped ROSCO, warm-started from the PI policy, and report a 15.7\,\% improvement in power regulation on a test wind profile and 30.5\,\% on a sawtooth profile. That comparison illustrates this paper's subject: the regulation metric is the standard deviation of a min--max-normalised power signal over a single 400\,s trace per profile, the structural loads are reported as channel statistics rather than damage-equivalent loads, and the ROSCO compared against is the shipped configuration. Their paper also contains an ablation that matters here: a soft-actor--critic agent trained from scratch under the same conditions regulates power \emph{worse} than the baseline it was meant to improve, which agrees with \citet{Nilsen2026expert}, who find a from-scratch learned wind controller starting about 12\,\% below its baseline until it is warm-started from a model-based expert.

\paragraph{Tuning the baseline is a published problem.} The corner frequency of ROSCO's shipped speed filter follows a generic rule of thumb~\citep{Abbas2022rosco}. \citet{Zalkind2020tuning} tune exactly this layer automatically, by zeroth-order optimisation over aeroelastic simulations, and report 20--26\,\% cost reductions over the default parameters in their pitch-control case on this same reference turbine. That work is the classical side of the observation this paper makes from the comparison side.

\paragraph{Residual policies, and where they may act.} Learning a bounded correction on top of a classical controller is standard, and restricting when the correction may act is part of that construction. \citet{Abbas2026specialized,Abbas2023specializedarxiv} apply a residual policy on a PID expert only in ``critical'' conditions identified by an input--output hidden Markov model trained offline, on the Tennessee Eastman process; \citet{Kim2026stabilityaware} suppress a residual inside a tracking-error deadband on a base carrying a disturbance observer; \citet{Cao2026autosafe} compose a learned policy with a safety-structured base. Our gate uses an exogenous variable, the wind-speed estimate the base controller already computes, rather than a state inferred from the plant's response, and our base is tuned, where \citet{Abbas2026specialized} state that their PID ``serves as a safety net and baseline rather than the ultimate optimized design''.

\paragraph{Language-model agents that design rewards.} Eureka evolves reward code against a task score~\citep{Ma2024eureka}, Language-to-Rewards and Text2Reward map instructions to reward parameters and programs~\citep{Yu2023l2r,Xie2024text2reward}, DrEureka extends the idea to domain randomisation~\citep{Ma2024dreureka}, and agentic designers close the loop with semantic feedback~\citep{Lee2026rda}; the construction has reached control tasks~\citep{Cardenoso2025learnopt,Casas2026large,Guo2024controlagent,Rasheed2025llmcontrol}, including reward-\emph{weight} adaptation for a building energy plant~\citep{Wu2026llmrewardweight}. \citet{Chen2026llmranking} show that open-weight models rank controller gains correctly only 31.8--53.2\,\% of the time and conclude that simulator-based validation remains necessary, which is why every proposal here is verified in simulation before it is kept, and why we run a random search over the agent's own vocabulary as the matched control.

\paragraph{Evaluation conventions, and the closest precedent to this paper.} Short-term damage-equivalent loads at W\"ohler exponents 4 and 10~\citep{IEC61400}, aggregation over a Rayleigh wind distribution and an actuator duty cycle are the field's standard instruments; rotor-effective wind-speed estimation, on which our gate depends, has its own literature~\citep{Pamososuryo2025rews}. The question this paper asks has been put before, and well: \citet{Requate2020evaluation} observe that wind-turbine control evaluations differ in models, simulation scope and result metric, that ``details \ldots can change the validity of results greatly'', and propose a structured process, aligned with the V-model, that specifies requirements across stable operation, energy production and structural loads. Their case study, a cyclic individual-pitch controller on a 7.5\,MW turbine, demonstrates the point through the seed count: splitting twelve seeds per wind speed into two sets of six gives blade-root fatigue reductions of 17\,\% and 10\,\% at one wind speed and 4\,\% and 10\,\% at another, from which they conclude that the six realisations the standard asks for ``are not necessarily a sound basis for a fatigue life evaluation''. They also report the actuation their controller costs, a 188\,\% increase in pitch operating time, next to the loads it saves. Our contribution is complementary and narrower: where they structure the evaluation and demonstrate that its parameters matter, we hold one plant and one set of simulations fixed and price several specific choices against each other, including two they do not vary, the tuning state of the baseline and the aggregation convention, with intervals on each. Paired bootstrap intervals per wind speed are, as far as we found, not standard practice here, and we adopt them because the controllers compared are deterministic and the only source of variation is the wind realisation.

\section{Setup}

\subsection{Plant, wind protocol, objective, statistics}
\label{sec:objective}
The plant is the NREL~5\,MW onshore turbine~\citep{Jonkman2009nrel5mw} in OpenFAST~4.2.1~\citep{OpenFAST}, driven at a 10\,ms control step by ROSCO~2.10.5~\citep{Abbas2022rosco} through a patched ZeroMQ interface. The pairing reference throughout is the shipped ROSCO GSPI with peak shaving on, every other load-mitigation feature off and constant generator torque above rated.

\paragraph{Wind.} Seven mean hub wind speeds, 12 to 24\,m/s in steps of 2; TurbSim fields of 650\,s with the Kaimal spectrum, a power-law shear profile and turbulence characteristic~B of IEC~61400-1 edition~3~\citep{IEC61400}, from which 600\,s episodes are simulated, the first 20\,s discarded and the remaining 580\,s scored. Published work more often discards 100--300\,s, so we measured rather than assumed: the tower-base moment's RMS in the first 20\,s window is 6.9\,\% above its settled level and in the second 0.9\,\%, and lengthening the discard from 20 to 100\,s moves the baseline's tower fatigue by $-0.5$\,\%, its speed variance by $-2.5$\,\% and its power MSE by $-4.2$\,\%. Those are shifts of the level that both controllers of a comparison share, so they cancel in the ratios reported here. Seeds 1--2 are the supervisor's winds and the only winds any selection sees; seeds 3--6 (28 episodes) are held out; seeds 7--10 (28 episodes) were generated after every controller was frozen. Training uses 150\,s episodes of the same seven wind speeds at seeds 1--2; \emph{every} reported number is a 600\,s evaluation.

\paragraph{Objective.} We adopt the metric set of a published RL pitch-control study~\citep{Baseline2026} and reduce it to one scalar,
\begin{equation}
\J = \tfrac14\!\sum_{m\in\{P,\omega,T,B\}}\!\mathrm{clip}_{\pm100}\big(\Delta m\,[\%]\big)\;-\;20\,\max\!\big(0,\;E_{\mathrm{loss}}[\%]-1\big),
\label{eq:J}
\end{equation}
the mean of the per-episode percentage reductions, against paired GSPI, of power MSE, generator-speed MSE (both on above-rated steps labelled by hub wind speed), tower-base fore-aft and blade-root out-of-plane damage-equivalent load, minus a penalty for energy loss beyond 1\,\%. Fatigue is the short-term DEL from rainflow counting, $\big(\sum_i n_i S_i^m/n_{\mathrm{ref}}\big)^{1/m}$~\citep{fatpack,IEC61400}; every fatigue number is a ratio between two controllers on the same episode, so $n_{\mathrm{ref}}$ cancels.

Two properties of \eqref{eq:J} bear on what follows. Its two regulation terms are not independent: above rated the torque is constant, so power and speed carry much of the same information and their reductions move together, with Pearson correlation 0.94--0.95 on the MPC rows and 0.19--0.83 on the PI rows. We keep the set unchanged because it is the published set this work is measured against, and we report the consequence: scoring on the three remaining terms changes no controller's rank in this paper except two seeds of one arm 0.05 points apart. And \eqref{eq:J} does not price pitch activity at all, which §\ref{sec:duty} treats as a separate axis. Because a set mean can hide a redistribution across the envelope, training and selection use a per-wind variant $C_w$ that charges 20 points for every metric falling more than 1\,\% below the base at \emph{any} mean wind speed (App.~\ref{app:objective}).

\paragraph{How numbers are reported.} Every metric in this paper is a percentage reduction against the paired shipped GSPI, and \J\ is the mean of four of them, so \J, each term and every difference between controllers are all in percentage points; after this paragraph we call them \emph{points}. A reduction is written positive: 24.5 means the quantity fell by 24.5\,\%. Values are given to one decimal, the precision the smallest uncertainty in this paper supports, the one exception being §\ref{sec:windobs}, where a policy is compared with itself on the same episodes and the comparison resolves a hundredth of a point. Two uncertainties appear and they are not interchangeable: square brackets are a 95\,\% interval over wind realisations for one fixed controller, and $\pm$ is a standard deviation across training seeds. Neither contains the other.

\paragraph{Statistics.} All controllers here are deterministic given the wind field, so the uncertainty of a fixed controller is over wind realisations: differences are paired stratified bootstraps over episodes (strata = mean wind speed), quoted as 95\,\% intervals. Across training seeds we report every seed. Six turbulent seeds per wind speed is the de-facto floor in this literature, and \citet{Requate2020evaluation} show it can be too few: two disjoint sets of six gave them blade fatigue reductions of 17\,\% and 10\,\% for the same controller at the same wind speed. The held-out evidence here is eight seeds per wind speed, in two disjoint sets of four, and we report both sets separately throughout rather than pooling them. On the merged 56-episode set the intervals are 0.3 to 0.6 points wide, which is the same order as the spread across training seeds, about 0.3 points of standard deviation, and the intervals do not contain it.

\subsection{The controllers}
\label{sec:controllers}
The rungs of the ladder. Construction details are in App.~\ref{app:controllers}; what matters here is that each is selected on the supervisor winds only and evaluated on the same held-out episodes.

\emph{Shipped GSPI.} The reference of \eqref{eq:J}, scoring 0 by construction. Enabling ROSCO's own tower fore-aft damper on it, at its shipped gain, is worth $-0.05$ points on this protocol; we did not tune that gain, so the row says what the shipped damper does here and nothing about a tuned one.

\emph{Tuned GSPI.} The shipped speed-filter corner frequency is 1.57\,rad/s and follows a generic rule of thumb. A search over that corner and a multiplier on the proportional gain, on the supervisor winds only, gives 3.42\,rad/s with the gain scaled by 1.13. Two parameters is a conservative amount of tuning; a full automatic tuning~\citep{Zalkind2020tuning} would leave the later rungs less room, not more.

\emph{LPV-MPC.} Three states, horizon 2\,s, re-linearised every 0.1\,s from the $C_P$/$C_T$ tables, solved with OSQP~\citep{Stellato2020osqp} in 3.6\,ms. Three design points matter and are developed in App.~\ref{app:mpc}: the cost terms are normalised to $O(1)$; a 5\,s output-disturbance estimate makes it offset-free, which is what absorbs aerodynamic model error; and the tower weight is scheduled on the wind estimate, from 3 below 18\,m/s to 6 by 22\,m/s, which keeps every term non-negative per wind speed at a cost of 0.6 points. The \emph{scheduled MPC} is the strongest controller here.

\emph{Learned layer.} Two PPO~\citep{Schulman2017ppo} policies, one per operating region, add a bounded collective-pitch offset to the base controller's target, second-order damped, limited to 0.0744\,rad below rated and 0.05\,rad above; the pitch limits apply to the sum, so with a zero residual the controller \emph{is} the base. The residual's increment is multiplied by a gate on a 10\,s low-pass of the controller's own wind-speed estimate,
\begin{equation}
\Delta\beta_t \;\leftarrow\; g(\hat v_t)\,\Delta\beta_t ,\qquad g(v)=\mathrm{clip}\big((v-15)/2,\,0,\,1\big),
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
present during training so that selection sees it. §\ref{sec:gate} prices the threshold.

\emph{Which wind signal each part reads} matters for how these results should be read, so we state it exactly. The MPC and the gate \eqref{eq:gate} both use the controller's own low-passed wind-speed estimate, which is available on a machine. The policy's observation vector, however, carries the simulator's true hub wind speed, and on this plant that is not a harmless substitution: over the baseline episodes the estimate has a $-0.35$\,m/s bias, a 1.42\,m/s error standard deviation and a correlation of 0.73 with the truth. The learned layer therefore had information a turbine does not have. §\ref{sec:windobs} measures how much of its margin depends on it, and the answer is at most 0.19 points of \J.

\emph{Reward supervision.} Every 32 main-trajectory episodes a reasoning language model receives the training diagnostics, an evaluation, the decision history and the objective, and returns $K=3$ candidate reward expressions; each is trained for one eight-episode fork, and the best continues. Fork episodes are charged to the same 300-episode budget, so a supervised run spends 180 episodes on its main trajectory and 120 in verification, and its total simulated time matches the unsupervised control to within 3\,\% (App.~\ref{app:compute}). The controls are a fixed, once-tuned reward and a random search over a fixed grammar of the agent's own term shapes under the identical loop. §\ref{sec:proposer} prices the proposer.

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} reads from the bottom up. Tuning one corner of the shipped baseline is worth 15.4 points at 1.14$\times$ its pitch travel, and the tower damper the toolbox provides is worth nothing at its shipped gain. The MPC adds another 13.1 points at twice the baseline's travel, buying them from regulation (power MSE 23.2 $\to$ 42.0) and from tower fatigue (5.4 $\to$ 20.9). Offset-free correction adds a further 1.0 point but is a trade, more regulation for less tower fatigue; its real value is that it makes the controller indifferent to a 5\,\% aerodynamic coefficient error, which costs the nominal controller 0.7 points.

Table~\ref{tab:top} and Fig.~\ref{fig:stack} give the top of the ladder on winds nothing in the pipeline had seen. \section{What the reporting choices are worth}

\subsection{The baseline}
\label{sec:baselever}
The largest single lever is applied before any advanced controller is built. Of the 32.3 points the full stack reaches over the shipped controller on the fresh seeds, 15.9 are the re-tuned baseline: 49\,\% of the total, against 45\,\% for the model-based controller and 6\,\% for learning. A study that compares a learned controller with the shipped default and reports 32 points has attributed half of them to learning.

None of this makes the shipped controller a straw man. It is the industrial reference and has been ported to a production programmable logic controller and verified against its native implementation~\citep{Corredera2026plc}. The point is narrower: its speed-filter corner is a generic rule of thumb, tuning it is published practice~\citep{Zalkind2020tuning}, and on this protocol that one number is worth more than most learned controllers report.

Since the point of this section is that the aggregation decides the answer, the aggregations are defined rather than described. Write $X^{C}_{s}$ for a quantity of controller $C$ on episode $s$, $\mathcal{S}_u$ for the episodes at mean wind speed $u$, $\mathcal{U}$ for the seven evaluated wind speeds and $w_u$ for a weight over them. The per-episode convention, which \eqref{eq:J} and every figure use, is
\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{ep}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left(1-\frac{X^{C}_{s}}{X^{B}_{s}}\right),
\label{eq:agg-ep}
\end{equation}
the mean of ratios taken episode by episode against the paired baseline $B$. The level convention aggregates the quantity itself, first within each wind bin and then across bins, with the exponent the quantity requires ($m=1$ for a mean square, the W\"ohler exponent for a damage-equivalent load):
\begin{equation}
A_m(X,w) = \left(\frac{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}} w_u\,\bar{X}_u^{\,m}}{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}} w_u}\right)^{1/m},
\qquad \bar{X}_u=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}_u|}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}_u} X_s,
\qquad R_{\mathrm{lv}}(w) = 1-\frac{A_m(X^{C},w)}{A_m(X^{B},w)} .
\label{eq:agg-lv}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:agg-lv} with $w_u\equiv1$ is the equally weighted level ratio; with $w_u$ the Rayleigh probability of bin $u$,
\begin{equation}
w_u \;\propto\; \exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\big(\tfrac{u-\Delta/2}{V_{\mathrm{ave}}}\big)^{2}\Big)-\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\big(\tfrac{u+\Delta/2}{V_{\mathrm{ave}}}\big)^{2}\Big),
\qquad \Delta=2\,\mathrm{m/s},
\label{eq:agg-ray}
\end{equation}
renormalised over $\mathcal{U}$, it is the conditional above-rated site weighting. The damage-equivalent load inside $X$ is itself $\big(\sum_i n_i S_i^{m}/n_{\mathrm{ref}}\big)^{1/m}$ over the rainflow-counted cycles of one episode, so $m$ enters twice, once within the episode and once across bins, which is why §\ref{sec:wohler} varies it.

Table~\ref{tab:conventions} holds the controllers and the episodes fixed and changes only which of these is applied. Two results stand out, and they point in opposite directions.

\emph{The model-based controller's regulation advantage disappears.} On power MSE it is $+24.6$ points as a mean of per-episode reductions, with a tight interval, and $-0.3$ $[-1.7,+1.6]$ under the site-weighted level aggregation. This is not a reversal, since the interval contains zero, and it should not be reported as one; it is an advantage that is large and well-measured under one convention and indistinguishable from nothing under another. The mechanism is visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:perwind}: a ratio of aggregated levels is dominated by the episodes with the largest error, which are the near-rated ones, and a Rayleigh weighting puts most of the probability there as well, while the MPC earns its regulation margin at high winds that are both easy in absolute terms and rare.

\emph{The same controller's fatigue advantage triples.} Tower-base DEL moves from $+10.3$ to $+18.1$ to $+29.0$ points across the same three conventions, every interval excluding zero, because tower fore-aft fatigue is dominated by the near-rated bins where the scheduled tower weight acts. Read over classes I to III the reduction grows further, 40.4, 43.8 and 46.1\,\%, while the speed-MSE reduction falls, 42.3, 34.8 and 29.5\,\%, since a lower annual mean shifts weight towards rated.

Neither convention is wrong. A mean of per-episode reductions answers what happens in a typical episode; a ratio of aggregated levels answers what happens in total, which is the question a fatigue calculation asks; a Rayleigh weighting answers what a site sees. What is not defensible is to report one without saying which, because on this plant they differ by a factor of three on one metric and by the whole effect on another.

\paragraph{A caveat on the site reading.} The evaluated bins span 12--24\,m/s and the Rayleigh weights are renormalised over them, so these are conditional above-rated aggregations rather than annual site fatigue. Below-rated operation carries a large part of the probability mass and its own fatigue; for the gated layer it is unchanged by construction, and for the MPC rows that is an assumption.

\label{sec:wohler}
The sensitivity above is specific, not universal, and the clearest way to show it is a choice that turns out not to matter. Repeating the whole fresh-seed comparison at tower $m=3,4,5$ and blade $m=8,10,12$, with the baseline side recomputed from the stored raw channels so that no simulation is repeated, leaves the ordering of the four controllers unchanged in all six columns, with one tie at tower $m=3$ (App.~\ref{app:wohler}). A learned layer on a strong base has a design choice with no counterpart in the classical controllers: the part of the envelope it is allowed to touch. We priced it by training the same layer with the gate opening at 13\,m/s instead of 15, two seeds on each base, giving three settings: none, 13--15 and 15--17 (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}).

The permissive direction is the wrong one on the objective: the setting used elsewhere in this paper is the best of the three on both bases and on both wind sets, and on the MPC base the ordering is monotone in the threshold. That ordering is consistent but the differences, 0.4--0.6 points, are of the size of the seed spread measured in §\ref{sec:objective}, so it is the pattern across four cells rather than any one of them that carries it.

Where each arm does its damage is the second reading, and it has to be counted carefully. A trained policy contributes two rows to Table~\ref{tab:sweep}, one per wind set, and those rows are not independent: the weights are the same and where a policy degrades is a property of the policy. Counting rows would therefore be pseudo-replication, so we count policies. At four low-gate policies the test is not conclusive and we do not present it as one. The low gate also widens the outcome: its two MPC-base seeds are the best and the worst of the seven MPC-base runs here, $+2.2$ and $+0.5$ on the fresh set. Two seeds per low-gate cell is a coarse sweep and we do not claim 15--17 is optimal.

\subsection{What the policy is allowed to know}
\label{sec:windobs}
A learned controller can be given signals a real one does not have, and the effect of that is a reporting choice like
any other. In our construction the MPC and the gate read the controller's own low-passed wind-speed estimate, but the
policy's observation vector carried the simulator's true hub wind speed. The two are not interchangeable here: over
the baseline episodes the estimate has a $-0.35$\,m/s bias, a 1.42\,m/s error standard deviation and a correlation of
0.73 with the truth, so the estimate is a substantially noisier signal than the one the policy was trained on.

We therefore re-evaluated the frozen policies with the estimate substituted into that slot, changing nothing else and
retraining nothing. This answers the narrower of the two questions one might ask, whether the oracle is load-bearing
at run time, and not the broader one, what a policy trained on the estimate from the start would reach; the latter
needs a retraining campaign we did not run.

The oracle turns out not to be load-bearing. This is the one comparison in the paper reported to a hundredth of a
point, because it puts one policy against itself on the same episodes and the intervals are a few hundredths wide.
Across the six gated policies the substitution costs $-0.09$ points of \J\ on average and $-0.19$ at worst, and one
policy on the tuned baseline improves slightly, by $+0.05$; these are real effects and they are small ones. Against margins of $+1.85$ and $+4.41$ points for the layer itself, and against a seed-to-seed
spread of $0.30$, a signal with 1.42\,m/s of error is worth two to ten per cent of what the layer earns. Pitch travel
is unchanged to two decimals in every policy.

\subsection{What no convention prices: actuation}
\label{sec:duty}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=0.56\linewidth]{f5_duty.png}
\caption{\J\ against pitch travel on the fresh seeds. Filled markers: the rungs of Table~\ref{tab:top}; open markers: the remaining seeds of each learned arm.}
\label{fig:duty}
\end{figure}
Every margin in this paper is bought with pitch activity, and \eqref{eq:J} charges nothing for it. The tuned baseline uses 1.14$\times$ the shipped controller's pitch travel, the MPC rows 1.97--2.14$\times$, and the gated layer a further 25--45\,\% on top of its base (Fig.~\ref{fig:duty}). As the actuator duty cycle $\mathrm{ADC}=\frac1T\int|\dot\beta|/\dot\beta_{\max}\,\mathrm{d}t$ with the controller's rate limit $\dot\beta_{\max}=10^\circ$/s, the fresh-seed values are 0.048 shipped, 0.054 tuned, 0.100 for the scheduled MPC, 0.067--0.070 for the tuned baseline with the gated layer and 0.129--0.149 for the MPC with it: an order of magnitude below saturation. That is the weaker of the two things one wants to know, because pitch-bearing and drive fatigue accumulate with travel whether or not the actuator is near its limit, so the duty \emph{ratio} is the cost that matters. The activity is broadband rather than concentrated at the blade-passing frequency: a 3P notch removes the spectral line, cuts travel by 2--3\,\% and costs 1.5--2.4 points. A search over the MPC's own weights at fixed duty has its knee at about 3$\times$ the shipped baseline's travel, and a controller selected at that knee keeps the same objective at 38--41\,\% of the actuation of the \J-selected point, at a cost of 3 points of blade fatigue. None of this is peculiar to a learned layer: \citet{Requate2020evaluation} report that the individual-pitch controller in their case study increases pitch operating time by 188\,\% while reducing blade fatigue, and count pitch cycles by amplitude for exactly this reason. An operator who prices actuator life will not read 32.3 as obviously better than 30.5.

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{What this implies for reporting.} Four recommendations follow, in decreasing order of what they are worth on this plant. Report against a baseline that has been tuned, and say how; here that alone is half the apparent benefit. State the aggregation convention and, where a fatigue claim is made, give both a per-episode and a level-based reading, since they differ by a factor of three here. If a site-weighted number is given, say over which bins it is normalised, because a weighting restricted to above-rated winds is a conditional statement and not an annual one. And report actuator duty next to every objective value, because none of the conventions examined here prices it. Two things we checked and found inert are the W\"ohler exponent and, within half a point, the length of the discarded start-up transient; we report them because a sensitivity study is only informative if it also contains choices that turn out not to matter.

\paragraph{What the layers are worth, stated plainly.} On a tuned PI base and with no aerodynamic model, a gated learned layer is worth $+4.4\pm0.3$ points at 1.40--1.45$\times$ the shipped controller's travel, in every seed and on fresh winds. A correctly scaled MPC is worth about three times that at twice the travel, and absorbs a 5\,\% aerodynamic coefficient error through its own estimator better than any residual we trained (App.~\ref{app:mpc}). On top of that MPC, learning is worth $+1.9\pm0.3$ points for 40\,\% more actuation. Which of these is the right controller is a question about the site and the actuator, not about the objective.

\paragraph{Limitations.} (i)~One onshore turbine, collective pitch, above rated, one turbulence class. The blade-root result is what collective pitch can be expected to give, since the once-per-revolution blade load needs individual pitch; an individual-pitch version of this layer was evaluated and is a negative result. (ii)~The tuning margin is specific to this machine and envelope: a shipped filter corner is conservative partly because it must serve turbines we do not simulate, floating ones in particular, where raising regulation bandwidth can drive negative damping of the platform pitch mode. (iii)~Our tuned baseline is tuned in two parameters, not optimised; against a fully tuned classical controller~\citep{Zalkind2020tuning} every margin here would be smaller. (iv)~Three training seeds per learned arm and two for the fixed-reward control; the paired intervals cover the wind realisation, and the seed-to-seed spread is of the same size. (v)~The gate threshold is swept at three settings with two or three seeds each. (vi)~The proposer result is established on the ungated tuned-PI base only. (vii)~The policies were trained with the simulator's true hub wind in their observation, not the controller's estimate; substituting the estimate at evaluation costs at most 0.19 points (§\ref{sec:windobs}), but a policy trained on the estimate from the start was not run. (viii)~Everything is simulation, and pitch duty is a proxy for bearing and drive life rather than an actuator model.

\section{Conclusion}

We built a ladder of pitch controllers on one turbine, evaluated every rung on the same held-out simulations, and then held the controllers fixed and varied the conventions under which they are reported. Of the 32.3 points the full stack reaches over the shipped controller, a two-parameter re-tuning of the baseline accounts for 15.9, the model-based controller for 14.5 and learning for 1.9. Changing only the aggregation and the wind weighting takes the model-based controller's power-regulation advantage over the tuned baseline from $+24.6$ points to an interval containing zero, and at the same time triples its tower-fatigue advantage from $+10.3$ to $+29.0$. The W\"ohler exponent changes nothing, which is how we know the sensitivity is specific.

The consequence is not that these comparisons are meaningless, but that a single number is not a result until the baseline, the aggregation, the weighting and the actuation are stated with it. The same accounting applies to the design choices inside a learned layer: where the layer is allowed to act is worth more than how its reward is tuned, and the language model supervising that reward is worth nothing on performance and something on constraint satisfaction. Among the configurations examined, and under the present simulation metrics, the most defensible performance--complexity trade-off is the one that needs no aerodynamic model: a tuned PI with a gated learned layer, 15.9 to $20.3\pm0.3$ points at 1.40--1.45 times the shipped controller's pitch travel. Calling that a deployment recommendation would require an actuator model and a bearing-life measure, neither of which is in this study.

\section{The per-wind constraint and the tuned fixed reward}
\label{app:objective}
The training objective is the per-wind variant of \eqref{eq:J},
\begin{equation}
C_w \;=\; \J \;-\; 20\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{m}\max\!\big(0,\;-1-\Delta m_u\,[\%]\big),
\label{eq:Cw}
\end{equation}
over the seven mean wind speeds $\mathcal{U}$ and the four metrics $m$, each evaluated against the run's own base controller on the same episodes: a metric that falls more than 1\,\% below the base at any single wind speed is charged, so a controller cannot pay for a gain at one wind speed with a loss at another. The set-mean version is not sufficient. In an earlier diagnostic on a different, lower-turbulence wind set (8, 12.5 and 15\,m/s, which is not the protocol of §\ref{sec:objective}), a residual trained under the set-mean form improved tower fatigue by 13.5\,\% at 8\,m/s while losing 17.5\,\% at 15\,m/s and still scored well.

The fixed reward used as a control is
\begin{equation}
r_t = \mathbf{1}[\text{Region 2}]\,w_P\!\left(\tfrac{P}{P_{\mathrm{base}}}-1\right)
      - \mathbf{1}[v_{\mathrm{hub}}>v_{\mathrm{rated}}]\,w_\omega\!\left(\tfrac{\delta\omega}{0.005}\right)^{2}
      - \lambda_T\,\ell_T - \lambda_B\,\ell_B - \lambda_A\,(\Delta\beta/\kappa)^2 ,
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
with $w_P=220$, $w_\omega=25.2$, $\lambda_T=1.0$, $\lambda_B=1.31$, $\lambda_A=0.1$, $\kappa=0.1$\,rad, and fatigue proxies $\ell_T,\ell_B$ given by the growth of the 10\,s trailing peak-to-peak range of tower acceleration and blade-root moment (the instantaneous moment and its increment both destabilise training); the speed term is gated by the wind label. The coefficients come from one fork search over the six knobs on the supervisor winds and are held fixed in every run that uses this reward.

\section{Controllers and training}
\label{app:controllers}
\paragraph{Tuned baseline.} A grid over the speed-filter corner frequency and a multiplier on the proportional gain, scored by \eqref{eq:J} on the supervisor winds only: 3.42\,rad/s (shipped 1.57) and $\times1.13$.

\paragraph{Learned layer.} PPO with $\gamma=0.998$ (a 0.99 discount is myopic with respect to the $\approx3$\,s tower mode and every method fails with it), GAE $\lambda=0.98$~\citep{Schulman2016gae}, $64\times64$ tanh networks, critic targets standardised by a running return-scale estimate~\citep{vanHasselt2016popart}, 300 episodes of 150\,s per run, eight parallel OpenFAST workers, so one training wave is eight episodes. Observations are generator-speed error and its rate, measured pitch, hub wind speed, blade-root out-of-plane moment, tower-top fore-aft acceleration and the base's target. The region is decided by the base controller's own pitch command: above rated once it has exceeded $0.5^\circ$ continuously for 1\,s, and below rated once it has fallen back under that threshold for as long. The reported checkpoint is always the best by $C_w$ on the supervisor winds; the last checkpoint is frequently degraded and is never used.

\paragraph{Supervisor.} The model is \texttt{gpt-5.6-luna} through an OpenAI-compatible endpoint, in JSON object mode, at reasoning effort \emph{medium} and a 6000-token completion limit; the provider rejects any temperature other than its default. Five to six calls are made per run. The random-structure control draws from a fixed grammar of the term shapes the agent itself has used, with log-uniform coefficients: speed terms $\{(\delta\omega/\sigma)^2,\;|\delta\omega|/\sigma,\;\tanh(|\delta\omega|/\sigma),\;\tanh((\delta\omega/\sigma)^2),\;\sqrt{|\delta\omega|/\sigma}\}$, load terms $\{\ell,\;\min(\ell,3),\;\tanh(\ell-1),\;\tanh\ell,\;\max(0,\ell-1),\;\log(1+\ell)\}$, power terms and an actuation term with or without saturation, under the same loop, the same $K$, the same wave length and the same budget.

\section{The model-based controller: design points and model error}
\label{app:mpc}
\emph{Cost scaling.} The three cost terms, on speed error, tower-top velocity and pitch increment, are each normalised to $O(1)$ at their typical magnitude. With the speed error normalised by rated speed instead, that term is $O(10^{-5})$ at a typical 0.5\,\% error while the tower term is $O(1)$; any tower weight then outweighs regulation by four orders of magnitude, the optimiser feathers the rotor, and the best such MPC carries no tower term at all and loses tower fatigue. Our own earlier implementation carried exactly that scaling and reproduced the familiar impression that MPC buys regulation with tower fatigue; on this plant that impression was an artefact of the normalisation. We make no claim about the published MPC literature, which generally reports load reductions.

\emph{Offset-free correction and model error.} A slow (5\,s) output-disturbance estimate on the speed channel makes the controller offset-free~\citep{Liu2024offsetfree}. With the power and thrust coefficients 5\,\% low in the internal model the nominal MPC loses 0.7 points over the range, the offset-free one loses nothing, and a recursive-least-squares variant is flat within 0.3 points over $\pm15$\,\% coefficient error. Learning is not the right tool for this: a residual trained across $C_P$/$C_T$ errors in $[0.85,1.15]$ repairs only the underestimate side, adds nothing at $+15$\,\%, and costs 3--6 points at the exact model, while residuals trained at one error do not transfer to another; the estimators are 10--40 points above every residual at every error. Learned model repair for this turbine has been reported offline~\citep{Anand2026adaptive}; the comparison here is the online one and it favours the estimator.

\emph{Wind-scheduled tower weight.} The tower weight that is right near rated is wrong at 22--24\,m/s, where the compensated controller's tower term turns negative. Raising the weight globally until that term is non-negative costs 2.2 points and 20\,\% more pitch travel, and larger weights destabilise 12\,m/s; under a per-wind load constraint the nominal controller (28.5) beats the constrained compensated one (27.3). Scheduling the weight on the wind estimate, from 3 below 18\,m/s ramping linearly to 6 by 22\,m/s, resolves it at a cost of 0.6 points. The construction is due to \citet{Wintermeyer2021weightsched}.

\section{Reward expressions written by the agent}
\label{app:rewards}
The expressions kept by the best run on each base, in the sandbox's variables (\texttt{region}: the router's region, 1 above rated; \texttt{region\_w}: the wind label; \texttt{d\_wg}: relative generator-speed error; \texttt{p\_ratio}: power over paired base power; \texttt{load\_t}, \texttt{load\_b}: the fatigue proxies; \texttt{act}: actuation). On the tuned baseline (fresh-seed \J\ 20.7):
\begin{small}\begin{verbatim}
45*tanh(p_ratio-1)*(1-region)
  - region_w*(36*tanh(abs(d_wg)/.004) + 10*tanh(abs(p_ratio-1)))
  - (1+2*region_w)*tanh(max(load_t-1,0)) - (1+region_w)*tanh(max(load_b-1,0))
  - .06*tanh(act)
\end{verbatim}\end{small}
and on the scheduled MPC (fresh-seed \J\ 32.5):
\begin{small}\begin{verbatim}
40*tanh(8*(p_ratio-1))*(1-region) - 22*tanh(180*abs(d_wg))*region_w
  - 10*tanh(8*abs(p_ratio-1))*region_w
  - (1+region)*tanh(max(load_t-1,0)) - 1.2*tanh(max(load_b-1,0)) - .2*tanh(act)
\end{verbatim}\end{small}
Three features recur and none is a coefficient of \eqref{eq:reward}: the regulation terms are saturated, so a single gust cannot dominate an episode's return; the fatigue terms are one-sided and bounded, charging only the excess of the proxy over its baseline value; and the tower term's weight is itself a function of the wind label. A fork search over the six coefficients of \eqref{eq:reward} cannot reach any of them, and never keeps a higher tower weight, because tower fatigue responds too slowly for a fork of this length to register it.
